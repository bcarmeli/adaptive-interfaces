# Beyond Natural Language

Consider a future where  artificial agents come to surpass humans in their capacity for reasoning and problem solving. Under this scenario, their internal cognitive processes may differ substantially from those of human reasoning, and need not be structured around the representational constraints that have shaped human cognition.

As humans increasingly expect artificial agents to assist through collaboration, coordination, and joint planning, new communicative demands emerge. This raises a foundational question: is natural language still the appropriate medium for machine intelligence, or is it shaped primarily by human cognitive constraints?

This question resonates with a long-standing philosophical intuition, famously articulated by Wittgenstein:  
“The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.”

Differences in internal reasoning and in the perceptual, computational and action-related affordances available to artificial agents are likely to manifest in developing communication. As artificial agents engage in collaborative problem solving, the forms of communicative protocols that best support coordination and shared understanding may diverge from those of natural language, reflecting the agents’ own cognitive structures and modes of interaction rather than those shaped by human constraints.

The central theme of this talk is that language evolves under pressure from two forces: affordance and need. Human language co-evolved with biological constraints such as limited memory, restricted articulation and embodied communicative affordances, alongside the demands of social coordination and cultural transmission. Yet even within human history, language has remained remarkably plastic, adapting to new affordances including writing and printing and, more recently, GPS-based communication and the widespread use of emojis in digital text, each enabling new forms of abstraction, compression, and efficiency.

Artificial agents inhabit a distinct landscape of affordances, shaped by the interaction between their perceptual, computational and action-related capacities and the environments in which they operate.. They can generate images with ease, maintain vocabularies far beyond human limits, represent and share precise spatial locations, and engage in parallel, multi-channel communication with a single interlocutor. At the same time, new needs arise. Autonomous systems must coordinate at timescales and levels of precision that natural language struggles to support. Scientific discovery and engineering design increasingly demand communication over abstractions that are awkward, verbose, or unstable in natural language. Finally, as AI systems grow increasingly complex and opaque, a related need emerges: humans seek protocols capable of exposing and interpreting the structure of machine reasoning, highlighting gaps in natural language for advanced coordination and understanding.

Against this backdrop, we explore the emerging idea of languages beyond natural language:  
task-oriented communication systems that are better aligned with machine intelligence. Drawing  
on recent work in emergent communication and multi-agent systems, we discuss evidence that  
artificial agents can develop protocols that outperform natural language in certain task-specific dimensions, such as efficiency and precision, while remaining unintelligible to human observers. These findings challenge the assumption that natural language is the privileged or necessary medium of intelligent communication.

Our aim is not to prescribe how artificial systems communicate, but to clarify the communicative possibilities that emerge once intelligence is no longer constrained by human affordances. Human language remains unparalleled socially, culturally, and emotionally, yet for artificial agents with distinctively non-human capabilities and affordances, it may no longer serve as the default substrate for advanced machine intelligence. Exploring this space philosophically, empirically, and ethically may be central to understanding the future of intelligence, particularly forms of intelligence that rely on communication, coordination, and shared representations. In such cases, the limits of a language may mark the boundaries of what can be jointly understood.

# Beyond Natural Language (v0)

Natural language has become the dominant interface between humans and artificial intelligence. Large language models now draw logical inferences, plan, and collaborate through linguistic structures shaped by human cognition, culture, and history. This success has encouraged an implicit assumption: that natural language is not only sufficient, but optimal, for intelligent interaction. In this talk, we argue that this assumption deserves re-examination.

As artificial agents increasingly interact with one another, and begin to surpass humans in perception, memory, and speed, a foundational question arises: is natural language still the right medium for machine intelligence? Or might it ultimately become a bottleneck, reflecting the constraints of the human brain rather than the needs of artificial systems?

This question echoes a long-standing philosophical insight, famously articulated by Wittgenstein:  
“The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.”

If language shapes what can be represented, reasoned about, and shared, then changes in language may signal changes in the structure of intelligence itself. While this idea has traditionally been applied to human cognition, it becomes newly urgent in the context of artificial agents whose epistemic affordances differ radically from our own.
